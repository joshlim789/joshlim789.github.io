Current work

Heterogeneous treatment effects with missing treatments

Some ICU patients receive a right-heart catheter and some don’t. Did it help — and for whom? The quantity of interest is the heterogeneous treatment effect,

\[\tau(x) = \mathbb{E}\big[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid X = x\big],\]

the causal effect for different kinds of patients rather than one population average. Estimating it normally requires knowing each unit’s treatment assignment. In practice that assignment is often partly missing: a health hazard recorded only for a subpopulation, an ad exposure that was never logged, a survey item nobody answered.

Standard practice splits the problem in two — impute the missing treatments, then estimate effects — which throws away the outcome’s information about who was likely treated and understates uncertainty in the result.

Gibbs-OMIT does both at once. A single Gibbs sampler alternates between fitting a flexible tree model (Bayesian Additive Regression Trees) for \(\tau(x)\), and re-imputing who was treated using the outcome as evidence.

The result is one posterior over both the effects and the missing treatments, so uncertainty about who was treated propagates into the final intervals rather than disappearing.

In simulation the method matches an oracle that observes every treatment (MSE 2.33 vs. 2.43), a 67% reduction against complete-case analysis (7.04), while MICE does worse than simply dropping incomplete rows (9.69). Interval coverage is 95%, against 79% for complete case and 88% for MICE.
